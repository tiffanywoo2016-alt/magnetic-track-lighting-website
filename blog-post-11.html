48V Track Load Planning: How Many Fixtures Per Run and How to Avoid a Costly Re-Work

By Tiffany Woo • June 24, 2026 • 5 min read

You quoted a project — 5m of track, 10 fixtures, single-end feed. Looks clean on paper. Installation day goes smoothly. Then the client walks in, looks up, and says: "The lights at the far end are darker than the ones near the wall."

Now you need to either:

(A) Add a second feed — cutting into the ceiling, extra labour, re-inspection.

(B) Replace the driver — which may not fix the issue if the voltage drop is in the track conductor, not the power supply.

(C) Explain to your client that the system was "designed for this effect."

None of these are good. Option A costs you time and reputation. And the fix was a 5-minute calculation at the quoting stage — before the ceiling was closed. This is why understanding 48V track power planning is one of the most valuable skills a contractor or specifier can develop.

Quick Answer

A single 48V magnetic track supports roughly 120–150W total from one feed point. The usable load depends on track length, fixture wattage and feed position — not just the driver rating.

Under 3m, under 100W → End feed works fine. Simple installation, no special planning needed.

3–5m, 100–150W → Use mid-point feed. Same track, same fixtures, but power connects at the center — halves the distance voltage needs to travel.

Over 5m or over 150W → Plan for dual-end feed or separate power zones. This is standard for commercial track lighting layouts with long ceiling runs.

Why 48V Track Voltage Drop Matters in Commercial Projects

Magnetic track lighting looks simple to install — clip modules onto a live rail and they light up. That simplicity hides a trap.

Each fixture draws power from the same copper conductor inside the track. As electricity travels down that conductor, some voltage is lost to resistance. The longer the run and the higher the total wattage, the more the low voltage track voltage drop becomes visible.

At the far end, the fixtures receive less than 48V. This means lower brightness, a slight colour shift toward warm, and potential flicker on dimmable setups. The frustrating part? The lights still turn on. The voltage drop magnetic track systems experience doesn't cause a shutdown — it produces an uneven result that looks unprofessional. And by the time anyone notices, the ceiling is closed.

For any commercial track lighting layout, this is a risk that should be eliminated at the design stage, not discovered during the client walk-through.

Think of It Like a Garden Hose — Pressure Drops as You Go Further

Imagine a garden hose with five spray nozzles attached along its length. Turn on only the first nozzle — water comes out strong. Turn on all five — the last one trickles. The water pressure dropped as it traveled, feeding each nozzle along the way.

48V track power feed behaves the same way. Voltage is highest at the feed point and drops gradually along the conductor. Understanding this relationship makes magnetic track power planning intuitive instead of theoretical.

The Three Levers That Control Load Capacity

1. Fixture Wattage — Simple Math, Often Overlooked

Every LED module has a wattage rating — 10W, 15W, 20W. The starting point is dead simple: add up every fixture on one track run.

A 10W spotlight + a 15W flood + a 12W grille = 37W total. Well within range. The problem happens when a long run has 15 fixtures at 15W each — that is 225W on one feed, and the last fixtures will be visibly dim regardless of the driver size.

For any track lighting load calculation, the math is the easy part. The hard part is anticipating total LED fixtures per track before the design is locked in — and accounting for how the load changes as modules are added or moved.

2. Feed Position — the Biggest Lever, Most Often Ignored

This is the single most impactful decision, and most project planners overlook it.

  • End feed — simplest installation. Voltage drops across the full length. Fine for short runs under 3m.
  • Mid-point feed — power enters at the centre. Voltage drops in both directions, so each side sees half the distance. Effectively doubles the usable load for the same track.
  • Dual-end feed — power at both ends. Voltage feeds from both sides toward the middle. Best for runs over 4m.
  • Zone separation — multiple power feeds on the same physical track, each feeding its own segment. Standard for large commercial installations.

Cost note: Adding a mid-point feed during initial installation costs almost nothing — just a junction box at the planned position. Adding it after the ceiling is closed costs 3–5× more in labour. This is the kind of detail that separates a smooth project from an expensive re-work.

3. Driver Headroom — the Common Mistake

A 120W load does not mean a 120W driver is enough. Drivers should run at 80–85% of rated capacity for efficiency and lifespan. A 150W driver is the right match for a 120W load. A 120W driver on a 120W load runs hot, ages faster, and may trip overload protection during startup.

This is essential magnetic track driver sizing knowledge that many first-time buyers miss. When in doubt, size up.

Real-World Configuration Reference for Magnetic Track Load Calculation

Setup Feed Type Result
2m track, 6×10W fixtures End feed ✅ Perfect — no voltage drop issue
3m track, 8×12W fixtures (96W) Mid-point feed ✅ Even across all fixtures
4m track, 10×12W fixtures (120W) Mid-point or dual-end ✅ Both work — dual-end gives more headroom
5m track, 12×12W fixtures (144W) End feed only ❌ Last 3–4 fixtures will be visibly dim
5m track, 12×12W fixtures (144W) Dual-end feed ✅ Even distribution, full brightness

How to Talk About This With Your Customer

"Think of it like a garden hose — the water pressure at the end depends on how many nozzles are open and how long the hose is. Same with a magnetic track. The voltage enters the track at one point, and as it passes each fixture, a tiny bit is used. Plan the feed position before installation, and every light will look identical."

This framing works for importers, wholesalers and contractors explaining 48V track system planning to their own clients. It turns a technical concept into something anyone can visualise.

What to Send Your Supplier — Save This Checklist

When you need a load check, send these 4 things. A supplier who asks for this information before quoting is someone who has dealt with rework before.

  • Track length per run (in metres)
  • Fixture types + wattages on each run
  • Ceiling type — determines whether junction boxes for mid-point feeds are possible
  • Dimmers or smart controls — DALI, 0-10V and Triac all have different driver matching requirements that affect magnetic track driver sizing

FAQ: 48V Track Load Planning

Can I add more lights later if I leave empty track sections?
Yes — but only if the remaining power capacity is enough. If you plan to expand later, oversize the driver from the start. It is far cheaper than upgrading later or running a second feed.
What happens if I exceed the track's maximum load?
The driver may shut down from overload protection, or the last fixtures on the run will be visibly dim with a noticeable colour shift. Neither is acceptable for a completed commercial installation. Re-working a closed ceiling to add a second feed is expensive.
Is 48V or 24V better for longer track runs?
48V is significantly better. Lower voltage means higher current for the same wattage, which means more voltage drop. For any run over 2m, 48V is the standard choice. This is one reason why 48V dominates the commercial track lighting layout market.
